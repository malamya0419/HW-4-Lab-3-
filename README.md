I loaded the Household Pulse Survey dataset and built a new variable, partnered, that marks anyone who is currently or was ever in a marriage-type relationship (married, widowed, divorced, or separated) as TRUE. I restricted the data to people aged 25–55 — old enough that most have finished their education, but not so old that retirement-age effects muddy the picture. Then I grouped by education level and calculated, for each group, the share who are married and the share who are divorced/widowed/separated. Lastly, I plotted education level on the x-axis, rate on the y-axis.


The marriage rate among those aged 25 to 55 typically rises with education, from 48% for those without a high school degree to 67% for those with a graduate degree. In contrast, the rate of those who are divorced, widowed, or separated averages about 20–26% for lower education levels, then reduces to about 11–12% for those with a college degree or more. In general, this implies that individuals with higher levels of education are more likely to be married and less likely to be separated, widowed, or divorced.
